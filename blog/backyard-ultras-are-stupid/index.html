Backyard Ultras Are Stupid

November 13, 2026

Backyard Ultras Are Stupid

Why have a finish line when you can just run around in circles until everyone gives up?

I have a confession. I don’t really understand backyard ultras.

Actually, that’s not entirely true. I understand the format perfectly well. You run a 4.167-mile (6.706km) loop, starting on the hour, every hour, until there’s only one person left who can complete a lap. That person wins. Everyone else gets a DNF.

And that’s precisely where my problem starts.

Backyard ultras seem to have developed this strange reputation where the expectation of failure is somehow part of the selling point. You’re almost certainly going to DNF. There are no second, third or fourth places. You either win, get an “assist”, or fail.

Sorry, what?

Why is this a good thing? Why would I actively seek out an event where the organisers are essentially telling me that I’m probably not going to finish?

I know, I know. It’s about the challenge. It’s about finding your limits. It’s about pushing yourself further than you ever thought possible.

But surely that’s what most ultra-running events are about anyway?

Anyway, I’ve got a few problems with the whole thing.

1. Apparently, There Are No Finishing Positions

One of the things you’ll regularly hear about backyard ultras is that there are no second, third or fourth places.

There’s the winner, the assist, and everyone else is a DNF.

The “assist” is the person who completes the same number of laps as the winner but doesn’t go out for the final, deciding lap.

I find this terminology a little daft, but we’ll get to that.

The argument is that a backyard ultra isn’t about coming second. It’s about being the last person standing.

Fine. But let’s not pretend there aren’t finishing positions.

If I complete 35 yards and finish ahead of someone who completes 34, I’ve finished ahead of them. If somebody completes 50 yards and I complete 30, they’ve completed 20 more yards than me.

We can dress it up however we like, but that’s a finishing position in everything but name.

Calling everyone a DNF doesn’t magically make the results disappear.

Perhaps it’s an attempt to create a different mindset, where the focus is on personal achievement rather than beating other people. I can understand that.

But I still think it’s a slightly ridiculous way of looking at a race.

2. You’re Only As Good As Your Competition

This is probably my biggest issue with the format.

In a traditional race, you can run for as long as you want, within the race rules and cut-offs. You might finish first, last or somewhere in between, but your performance is ultimately your own.

In a standard backyard ultra, that’s not really the case.

You can only keep going for as long as somebody else keeps going.

Let’s say I’m having the race of my life. I’m feeling fantastic, my legs are holding up, I’ve barely slept but I’m somehow still ticking off the yards.

I’ve got another 10, 20 or even 30 laps in me.

Unfortunately, the other remaining competitor decides they’ve had enough after 20 laps. They stop. I complete lap 21, and that’s it. Race over.

Regardless of whether I could have run another 10, 20 or 30 laps, I don’t get the opportunity.

I’ve effectively been limited by somebody else’s performance.

I’ve heard of some events, including formats being experimented with in Russia, where runners can continue for as long as they want, rather than being forced to stop when they’re no longer competing against anyone.

I actually quite like that idea.

Why not let people discover their own limits? Why should the person who finishes second dictate how far everybody else gets to run?

If the whole selling point is finding out just how far you can go, surely you should be allowed to find that out?

3. It’s Literally Running Around In Circles

I appreciate that this is probably the most obvious criticism, but hear me out.

One of the things I love about trail running is the variety. The scenery, the terrain, the climbs, the descents, the technical sections, the views and occasionally the feeling that you’ve accidentally signed up for a mountain rescue exercise.

Backyard ultras offer you a field and a short loop.

Then you run it. Again. And again. And again.

I understand that the repetition is part of the challenge. I understand that the simplicity makes it easier to organise and that you don’t need complicated logistics, extensive route marking or dozens of checkpoints.

But I can’t help wondering how many times I could run the same 6.7km loop before I started questioning every life decision that had brought me there.

Probably somewhere around lap three.

And yes, I appreciate that this is a personal preference rather than a particularly sophisticated criticism of the sport.

Still, I like my trail running to involve a little more trail.

4. Have We Reached Peak Backyard Ultra?

It feels like backyard ultras have absolutely exploded in popularity.

Everywhere you look, there’s another one popping up. Local events, regional events, national championships and all sorts of variations on the original concept.

And I can’t help wondering whether we’ve reached saturation point.

On the surface, they’re potentially quite attractive events to organise. You need a relatively short loop, a suitable field or venue, somewhere for runners to rest, and the people and infrastructure to keep everything ticking over.

Compared with organising a traditional ultra across 50 or 100 miles of trails, the logistics can look considerably simpler.

Of course, that doesn’t mean they’re easy to organise. Keeping an event running for potentially several days, managing runners, volunteers, safety and facilities is a serious undertaking.

But the commercial opportunity is obvious.

Take a format that has become increasingly popular, attach an entry fee, and you’ve potentially got yourself an event that doesn’t require a particularly complicated course.

And then there’s the entry fees.

Some backyard ultras seem surprisingly expensive for what is, essentially, a short loop repeated over and over again.

I appreciate that there are costs involved. Marshals, insurance, facilities, timing, food, equipment and all the other things that go into putting on a race. I’m not suggesting organisers should work for free.

But when the course is a relatively simple loop that runners repeat themselves, I do sometimes question where the money is going.

I’ve also started seeing events cancelled, and I wonder whether this is partly a symptom of a market becoming saturated.

Perhaps we’ve got to the point where there are more events than there are runners willing to enter them.

I don’t know whether that’s the case, but it would be interesting to see.

5. Is It All Just a Barkley Copycat?

I can’t help feeling that backyard ultras owe at least some of their popularity to the mystique surrounding the Barkley Marathons.

The Barkley is one of the most fascinating events in ultra-running. It’s notoriously difficult, deliberately unconventional and surrounded by an almost mythical reputation.

It’s an event where simply finishing is an extraordinary achievement.

And I wonder whether backyard ultras have tapped into some of that same appeal.

The idea of an event where almost nobody finishes. Where the challenge is as much mental as physical. Where simply taking part means you’ve signed up for something most people wouldn’t dream of attempting.

It’s a compelling story.

I have no evidence that backyard ultras were created as a commercial attempt to replicate the Barkley’s appeal, and I’m certainly not suggesting every organiser is sitting there rubbing their hands together at the thought of making a quick buck.

But I do wonder whether the popularity and mystique of events like the Barkley have helped create a market for increasingly unusual and extreme race formats.

And whether some people have spotted a commercial opportunity in that.

Perhaps that’s unfair. Plenty of organisers will be passionate runners who genuinely love the format and want to share it with others.

But when you see the number of events appearing, the entry fees being charged and the increasingly elaborate marketing around what is fundamentally a repetitive running format, it’s hard not to be a little cynical.

6. The Terminology Is Daft

One final moan.

“Yards”. “Assist”. “Last person standing”.

I know these terms have become part of the culture, and I know there’s a history and a set of traditions behind them.

But sometimes I think the terminology is trying a little too hard to make something relatively simple sound more mysterious and significant.

A lap is a lap. A DNF is a DNF. And if you finish second, you’ve finished second.

Perhaps I’m just being grumpy.

Actually, that’s quite likely.

Okay, But Let’s Give Credit Where It’s Due

Now, despite everything I’ve just said, I do have a huge amount of respect for anyone who takes on a serious backyard ultra.

Because when you look beyond the format, the physical and mental demands are genuinely extraordinary.

For starters, winning one can mean running 200 or 300 miles. Sometimes even further. That’s an absolutely ridiculous amount of running, regardless of whether you’re doing it on a mountain, through a forest or around a field.

And there’s a very different sort of challenge involved compared with a traditional ultra.

You can’t simply go out hard, bank some time and hope to hang on towards the end. You need to maintain a consistent, sustainable pace, lap after lap, hour after hour.

Go too quickly and you’re wasting energy. Go too slowly and you risk missing the next start.

Then there’s the sleep deprivation.

You might be running through the night, grabbing a few minutes of rest between laps, trying to eat enough to keep going and somehow convincing your body that another 6.7km is a perfectly reasonable thing to do.

And that’s before you get to the mental side of things.

Imagine waking up every hour, knowing you’ve got to get yourself back out there. No big finish line to look forward to, no dramatic final descent, no ticking off the last few miles of a race. Just another lap.

And then another.

And another.

The ability to manage your nutrition, pacing, sleep, recovery and mindset over potentially several days is something I genuinely admire.

There’s also something to be said for the community around these events. Runners supporting each other, sharing resources, helping each other through difficult moments and creating an atmosphere that can be quite different from a conventional race.

I might not particularly fancy running one myself, but I can absolutely appreciate why people love them.

And perhaps that’s the most important point: just because I don’t understand the appeal doesn’t mean there isn’t one.

So, Are Backyard Ultras Actually Stupid?

Probably not.

Well, maybe a little bit.

I still don’t understand why having a guaranteed DNF is something to celebrate. I still think the whole “no second place” argument is a bit silly. I still think running around the same loop for days on end sounds like a particularly creative form of torture.

And I remain unconvinced that we need quite so many of them.

But I also recognise that running potentially 300 miles, managing sleep deprivation, maintaining a consistent pace and battling your own mind for days on end is an incredible achievement.

The people who do it deserve enormous respect.

Perhaps I’m simply not the target audience.

I’ll stick to my traditional trail ultras, where I can enjoy some scenery, climb a mountain, get lost occasionally and eventually cross an actual finish line.

But if you fancy running around a field 70 times, surviving on biscuits and caffeine, and then calling yourself an “assist” rather than coming second, crack on.

I genuinely admire you.

I just think you’re all a little bit mad.

These are personal opinions, not expert analysis. I’ve never competed in a backyard ultra, so I’m more than happy to be educated, challenged or told I’m talking nonsense. That’s rather the point of Trail Takes.
