Ultra Running Has Become a Rich Person’s Hobby

There’s something quite ironic about ultra running.

It’s supposed to be a sport where you strip everything back. You, a pair of shoes, a trail, and the small matter of running an absolutely ridiculous distance.

No fancy stadiums. No expensive machinery. No need for a team of specialists. Just get out there and keep moving.

Except, have you seen the cost of getting involved lately?

Between race entries, shoes, GPS watches, poles, mandatory kit, nutrition, travel and accommodation, ultra running is starting to look less like a simple sport and more like an expensive lifestyle subscription.

And I’m beginning to wonder whether we’ve created a sport where having enough money is becoming almost as important as having enough endurance.

The Entry Fee Is Just the Beginning

Let’s start with the obvious one: race entries.

Some ultra events now command seriously hefty entry fees. And I appreciate that organising an event isn’t cheap. There are permits, insurance, medical cover, checkpoint supplies, volunteers, timing systems, transport and all the other things that runners probably don’t give a second thought to when they’re happily stuffing their faces at an aid station.

I’m not suggesting race organisers should work for nothing.

But when you’re looking at spending a substantial amount just to get a place on a start line, you have to wonder where it’s all heading.

And the entry fee is only the beginning.

If the event is several hours from home, there’s travel. If it’s a multi-day event, there’s accommodation. You might need to take time off work. You might need someone to support you.

Suddenly, that £150 race entry can become a considerably more expensive weekend.

And if you’re doing several events a year, the costs quickly add up.

Apparently, You Need £1,000 Worth of Kit to Run Through a Forest

Have you ever looked at the kit some runners carry?

A GPS watch that costs more than a decent second-hand car. Carbon-plated shoes. Expensive trail shoes for different terrain. Running poles. Technical waterproofs. Specialist running packs. Head torches that could illuminate a small village.

And that’s before we get to the nutrition.

Gels, powders, electrolytes, recovery drinks, protein products, caffeine products and whatever new miracle supplement has appeared on Instagram this week.

I’m not saying any of these things are useless. Far from it. Good equipment can make a genuine difference, particularly when you’re spending 20, 30 or 40 hours out on the trails.

But there’s a difference between having the equipment you need and feeling that you need to buy every new bit of kit that the ultra running world tells you is essential.

You can run an ultra without the latest GPS watch. You can run a mountain trail without carbon-plated shoes. And you don’t necessarily need a nutrition strategy that looks like a pharmaceutical research project.

Sometimes, a decent pair of shoes, a waterproof jacket and a bit of common sense will do.

Or at least, they used to.

The Instagram Effect

I think social media has played a part in this.

Scroll through Instagram and you’ll see runners with immaculate kit, expensive watches, carefully arranged nutrition, stunning race photography and sponsorship deals with brands you’ve probably never heard of.

Everything looks professional. Everything looks aspirational.

And that’s fine. If someone enjoys buying the latest equipment and can afford it, crack on.

But it can create the impression that this is what being an ultra runner looks like.

You need the right shoes. The right watch. The right nutrition. The right training plan. The right recovery tools.

Before long, the barrier to entry isn’t just the physical challenge of running 50 or 100 miles. It’s the financial investment required to feel properly equipped to do it.

I wonder how many people look at the sport and decide it’s simply too expensive to get involved.

That’s a shame.

Because the ability to run a long way shouldn’t depend on the size of your bank balance.

The Cost of Getting to the Start Line

There’s another cost that doesn’t get discussed quite as much: getting to the races in the first place.

Some of the most appealing ultra events take place in remote, beautiful locations. That’s part of their attraction.

But if you don’t live nearby, getting there can be expensive.

Flights, trains, petrol, hotels, camping, food and time off work all need to be factored in.

And if you’re travelling internationally for a race, the costs can become enormous.

It’s easy to look at a calendar full of exciting events and think about which ones you’d like to enter. It’s a different matter when you start adding up what it would actually cost to participate.

For some runners, choosing a race isn’t about finding the best event. It’s about finding one they can afford to get to.

And that can mean missing out on some fantastic experiences.

Are Race Organisers Part of the Problem?

This is where I need to be a little careful, because I don’t want to give race organisers an unnecessarily hard time.

Putting on a safe, well-organised ultra is a serious undertaking. Costs have increased across almost every area, and I wouldn’t expect anyone to organise an event at a financial loss.

But I do think the sport needs to be careful about how it develops.

Are entry fees increasing because events genuinely cost more to deliver? Or are we also seeing a market where organisers know that runners are willing to pay increasingly large sums for a place on a desirable start line?

Probably a bit of both.

There’s also a question about what we’re actually paying for.

Do runners want more elaborate race villages, expensive finishers’ merchandise, branded goodies and increasingly impressive production values? Or would many of us be perfectly happy with a well-marked course, decent checkpoints, good organisation and a finish line?

Personally, I’d take the latter.

I’d rather pay a sensible entry fee for a brilliantly organised event than spend a fortune on an experience that looks fantastic on social media but doesn’t necessarily deliver anything extra where it matters.

Not every ultra needs to be a festival.

Is This Making the Sport Less Accessible?

This is my biggest concern.

Ultra running should be one of the more accessible sports around.

You don’t need a football club, a swimming pool, a tennis court or a gym membership. You don’t need to be particularly wealthy to go out and run on a trail.

At its simplest, it’s just running.

But the organised ultra scene is becoming increasingly expensive, and I worry that we’re creating an unnecessary divide between people who can afford to participate regularly and those who can’t.

There’s a difference between choosing to spend money on a hobby and feeling that you have to spend money to keep up with it.

And there’s a danger that we start judging runners by their equipment, their race calendar or the events they’ve completed, rather than by the simple fact that they’re out there doing it.

Some of the best runners I’ve encountered won’t have the latest watch or the most expensive kit. They might not have a social media following or a collection of impressive race photographs.

But they’ll happily head out into the hills and run for hours.

That’s what I think ultra running should be about.

In Fairness…

I do appreciate that there’s another side to this.

Ultra running isn’t necessarily getting more expensive just because everyone involved is trying to squeeze more money out of it.

The costs of organising events are real. Insurance, permits, medical provision, staffing, equipment and transport aren’t free. In fact, the more remote and challenging an event becomes, the more expensive it can be to organise safely.

And some of the equipment we spend money on genuinely matters. A reliable waterproof, a decent head torch and appropriate footwear aren’t frivolous purchases when you’re heading into the mountains for a long period of time.

There’s also nothing wrong with treating running as a hobby and spending money on it. People spend thousands on bicycles, golf clubs, skiing holidays and all sorts of other interests. Why should ultra runners be any different?

If someone wants to spend £300 on a watch, £200 on shoes and a small fortune travelling around the world to run ridiculous distances, that’s entirely up to them.

I’m not suggesting we should all turn up to a 200-mile race wearing a pair of supermarket trainers and carrying a carrier bag full of sandwiches.

Well, not unless the event specifically allows it.

The point isn’t that spending money is bad. It’s that the sport shouldn’t become so expensive that people feel they can’t participate without spending it.

So, How Much Does It Really Cost to Be an Ultra Runner?

I don’t want ultra running to become a sport where the biggest investment is financial rather than physical.

I’d like to see more affordable events, less pressure to buy the latest equipment and a bit more recognition that you don’t need to look like a sponsored athlete to be a proper runner.

I’d also like to see us celebrate the local 50K runner just as enthusiastically as the person flying across the world to complete some exotic 200-mile race.

Because, ultimately, the trails don’t care what watch you’re wearing, how much your shoes cost or how many followers you’ve got.

They’ll still be there when your expensive GPS watch runs out of battery.

And you’ll still have to run up the bloody hill.

Strong opinions, questionable conclusions, and absolutely no guarantee that I’m right.
